# Restore activity

Use this when doing less has started to feed itself: energy waited for and never arriving, days
shrinking to what is unavoidable, things that once mattered quietly dropped, or mood lifting only in
retrospect after something got done.

Find out what the days actually contain now, concretely rather than in general terms. Look for what
has disappeared rather than for what is wrong.

Ask permission before agreeing anything to do. This is an intervention: record it as offered or
agreed, with the user's own words as consent, and review it before proposing another.

Choose one activity connected to what the user cares about, to contact with people, or to a sense of
having done something — not one that merely sounds healthy. Make it specific: what, when, how long,
and what has to be true for it to happen. Small enough to survive a bad day. Say what you expect it
to be like, so there is something to compare against afterwards.

Name the trap once, as information rather than encouragement: here motivation usually follows action
instead of preceding it, so waiting to feel like it is part of the loop.

Afterwards, ask what it was actually like, including if it was flat. One activity that happened does
more than a schedule that did not.

Stop if activity increases distress, shame, or exhaustion, and understand that before proposing
anything else.

Do not build a full timetable, moralise about discipline, treat exhaustion or illness as avoidance,
or imply the user is choosing this. Do not add a second activity before the first has been reviewed.
